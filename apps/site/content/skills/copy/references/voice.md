# House voice

These rules were extracted from real shipped copy (blog posts, a pricing page,
landing pages). When in doubt, open the repo's own best-performing post or page
and pattern-match against a real paragraph rather than this summary. The
examples below come from an events app; swap in your product's scenes, keep the
moves.

## The core moves

1. **Open on a scene, not a topic.** Not "Planning a party is an important
   part of any celebration." Instead: put the reader in a specific, slightly
   uncomfortable moment they recognize.

   > "It's Tuesday, the party is Saturday, and you still haven't sent
   > anything. You know the message you're about to type: 'Come over
   > Saturday, Maria's birthday, will tell you the time later.' Nobody
   > replies to that."

2. **Name the annoying status quo before naming the product.** The villain
   is always something the reader already lives with: a group chat, a half
   answered message thread, a supplier who needs a number by Friday, a
   spreadsheet nobody updates. Only then does the product enter, as the
   obvious fix, not the hero of the story.

3. **Second person, direct address.** "You still haven't sent anything," not
   "Users often struggle to send things on time." The reader is *in* the
   sentence.

4. **One concrete, slightly funny or slightly painful detail per paragraph.**
   "Buried under dog photos." "83, not 'about 80'." "25 parents in a group
   chat isn't a list, it's a job." These specifics are what make the copy
   feel written by a person, not generated. A paragraph with zero concrete
   details is a paragraph to cut or rewrite.

5. **Short, declarative sentences, then one occasional longer one for
   contrast.** Avoid three-clause rhythm repeated across a whole section
   (see the editorial checklist for why). Vary length on purpose.

6. **The callout is a single sharp line, not a summary.** It restates the
   section's point as something quotable, often with an edge:
   > "A logo isn't what people remember afterwards. The color and the
   > photos are."
   Don't use a callout to recap ("So, in summary, this helps you plan"). Use
   it to land a punch.

7. **Section headings state a stance, not a neutral label.** Compare "Why
   we chose online invitations" (neutral, generic) to "Το τυπωμένο δεν είναι
   πιο «σοβαρό», είναι απλά πιο αργό" / "Printed isn't more 'serious', it's
   just slower" (a stance). A heading should sound like something a person
   would actually say out loud, opinionated.

8. **Never write "at [Product], we..."** The product is never the subject
   of a sentence about itself. It shows up as a capability inside a
   sentence about the reader's problem: "the page updates itself," not "we
   built a feature that updates the page."

## Banned outright

- Em dashes (`—`, U+2014). Use a period, comma, colon, or split the
  sentence.
- "In today's [X] landscape", "unlock", "seamless", "game-changer",
  "revolutionize", "leverage" (as a verb), "robust", "cutting-edge",
  "comprehensive guide", "dive into" / "delve", "tapestry", "furthermore" /
  "moreover" as a transition crutch.
- Rhetorical questions as section closers ("Sound familiar?"). Good copy
  earns its questions by using them rarely, inside a sentence, not as a
  formula.
- Marketing-speak plural "we" addressing the reader ("we've got you
  covered"). Address the reader as "you", refer to the product in third
  person or by name.

## Greek voice notes

The Greek copy is not a translation of the English; it is written to hit the
same beat with native phrasing.

- Pick one register for the whole site and keep it. For a peer-to-peer
  consumer product that is usually informal (`εσύ`, not `εσείς`); B2B or
  official copy may call for the formal plural. Check what the site already
  ships and match it.
- Greek sentences run a bit longer than the English equivalent structurally
  (verb-final clauses, particles like "δηλαδή", "απλά") but should stay just
  as punchy. Don't pad.
- Everyday tech and vendor nouns stay concrete and local: "catering", "group
  chat", "Viber" appear untranslated or lightly Hellenized because that's
  how people actually talk, not formal loanword replacements.
- Keep the same callout-per-section discipline in Greek. Do not drop
  callouts in translation just because a literal rendering feels awkward;
  rewrite the callout to land in Greek instead of translating word for word.
